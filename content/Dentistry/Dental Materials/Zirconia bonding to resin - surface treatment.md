### In-text annotations

 <mark class="hltr-yellow">"Due to its high stiffness and chemical inertness to resist corrosion, it is very difficult to physically or chemically modify the surface of zirconia ceramic."</mark> [Page 2](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/TE77SP4F?page=2&annotation=J8XBVLFS)

<b>Stiffness</b> = high <b>elastic modulus (Young’s modulus)</b> Zirconia has a modulus of ~200 GPa (comparable to steel), which means: It <b>resists deformation</b> under stress. It’s <b>very rigid</b> and <b>brittle</b>, not flexible.

Due to its stiffness and hardness, zirconia resists abrasion: Zirconia <b>does respond to air-abrasion</b>, but <b>due to its high stiffness and hardness</b>, it requires <b>specific and optimized parameters</b> (like pressure, particle size, and duration) to be effective — more so than softer ceramics like feldspathic or lithium disilicate. You need higher energy or more aggressive methods to create enough micromechanical retention. 


<b>Doesn’t Flex or Conform</b> Flexible materials can deform slightly and “wet” adhesives better. Zirconia’s stiffness means it <b>won’t conform to or interlock easily</b> with adhesive resins, especially during polymerization shrinkage — leading to <b>stress concentration</b> and <b>weak interfacial bonding</b>. <b>Failure Prone Under Stress</b> The bond interface between a stiff material (zirconia) and a more flexible one (resin) can <b>delaminate or crack</b> due to the mismatch in mechanical properties. Stiffness creates <b>stress differentials</b> during loading (chewing) or thermal changes. [Page 2](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/TE77SP4F?page=2&annotation=J8XBVLFS)
