Posterior Prep Design For Lithium Disilicate



1) Occlusal reduction: Minimum 2.0 mm (3.0 mm even better). This provides enough room for strength of my ceramic restorations and allows elimination of lateral interferences.

2) Chamfer margins: Minimum 0.2 mm. Although we can press to a feather edge prep it is too fragile to handle in many cases. Bigger teeth get more reduction. Smaller teeth may get less.

3) Mesial and distal boxes: This provides retention for our provisionals and adds bulk of ceramic under marginal ridges where occlusal loading is significant. Many marginal ridges chip without a broad box form to add thickness to the ceramic.

4) Two plane facial and lingual cusp reduction: This minimizes "fat" crowns with thin zones over functional cusps and gives bulk to ceramics in the cosmetic zone of non-functional cusp tips. Many preps in the posterior fail to reduce the functional cusp in two planes sufficiently to allow easy placement of the cusp in the opposing fossa. This results in contact on inclines with damaging non-axial forces. In addition, lateral function has a squeak which patients hate. 

5) Two plane occlusal reduction: This provides thickness in the depth of the fossa areas where forces will be the greatest. Flat occlusal surfaces are simply wrong.

6) Smooth line angles: Rounding the line angles makes it easier for the lab to press and fit restorations to a precise fit. Very sharp line angles can cause fractures while cooling in the oven.

7) Minimize proximal concavities: Preparing the lingual aspect of the concavity more aggressively results in less of a niche for plaque to form and makes the hygienist's job much easier.  

— with James D. Free-cdt and Michael Brumm.
